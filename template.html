An open research experiment: what happens when public figures talk up an investment?

We track sourced investment mentions by Donald Trump, his family and administration officials, then compare the assets with the broader market. We also examine price moves before a comment and public records of related holdings and trades — patterns relevant to questions about conflicts of interest, insider trading and market manipulation.

Start with the results below, then explore the original statements, performance checks and public disclosures. Small, overlapping samples and incomplete filings limit what we can conclude. A timing overlap is not evidence of wrongdoing.

        Is this more than luck? (significance tests)

        Is this more than luck? (average excess vs , with a 95% range)

        For each time window, we resample the mentions 2,000 times (a "bootstrap") to see how much the average could swing by chance. If the 95% range crosses zero, the result can't be told apart from luck. The coin-flip check asks whether the share of mentions beating the index is unusually far from 50%. Small, overlapping samples make these ranges wide.

        Compared with ordinary days (random-date baseline)

        Compared with ordinary days (random-date baseline for the same assets, vs the S&P 500)

        A fair question about any jump is: "does this asset do that all the time?" Here each mentioned asset is compared with itself on ordinary days. For every asset we take its trading days from two years before the comment up to today, leave out the month either side of any tracked comment on it, and measure how it did against the S&P 500 on those days. Then we pretend to "mention" each asset on a random ordinary day, 2,000 times over, to see what a normal average looks like. If the real comment-day average sits outside that normal range, chance and everyday volatility are unlikely to explain it. Each mention counts separately here (the "count each post once" box doesn't apply), and it is always measured against the S&P 500.

        Adjusted for how jumpy each asset is (beta)

        Adjusting for how jumpy each asset is (beta-adjusted returns vs the S&P 500)

        Some stocks swing much more than the market (a "high-beta" stock might move 2% when the S&P 500 moves 1%). Simply subtracting the index can make such a stock look good in a rising market or bad in a falling one. Here each asset's typical sensitivity to the S&P 500 (its beta) is measured from about a year of daily prices ending roughly six weeks before the comment, and the expected market-driven part (beta × the S&P 500's move) is subtracted. What's left is the move the market alone doesn't explain. Always measured against the S&P 500, whichever benchmark is selected above.

        Did the stock beat its own industry?

        Did the stock beat its own industry? (vs sector fund, and vs a tighter industry peer ETF)

        A stock can beat the S&P 500 just because its whole sector was rising. First we compare each mentioned stock with its Select Sector SPDR fund (for example chip stocks vs the tech fund XLK). Then, where a tighter industry ETF exists, we also compare it with that peer (for example chips vs the semiconductor fund SMH, uranium vs URA, rare earths vs REMX). The peer is a closer yardstick for "did this company stand out from its own niche?"

The lawsuits allege subscribers get government news before everyone else; at the hearing the judge asked whether the feed is only about 50 milliseconds faster, and the defence argued a story arriving a bit later is not real harm.         How the ranking works (method & caveats)
        • Data: every current member's periodic transaction reports (House Clerk and Senate eFD), as compiled on Quiver Quantitative's public per-member pages. Only listed stocks (and ETFs) with a ticker and daily prices are scored; bonds, options, funds without tickers and private holdings are skipped.
        • Each trade's result: the stock's total return from the close on the trade date to 30 (or 90) calendar days later, minus the S&P 500 fund's (SPY) total return over the same days. A purchase scores that gap; a sale scores the opposite (selling before a stock lags counts as a good call).
        • Average vs S&P 500: equal weight per trade. $-weighted uses the midpoint of each disclosed dollar range (for example $1,001–$15,000 → $8,000), so large trades count more. Hit rate: share of trades with a positive score.
        • Vs peers: the member's average minus the average of all trades by members of the same chamber over the same period.
        • 95% range: a bootstrap that resamples whole trading days (not single trades), because members often file many trades on one day that move together. Members need at least 10 scored trades on at least 8 different trade dates to be ranked (with fewer dates the range can't be estimated).
        • Many tests: we test every member, so a few will look unusual by luck (about 1 in 20 at the 95% level). The Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment controls the share of false alarms among those flagged as "stands out".
        • Limits: filings lag trades by up to 45 days and give ranges, not amounts; trades by spouses, dependents and managed or blind accounts are mixed in; trade dates (not exact times) are used; former members and executive-branch officials are not included yet. Executive officials' and family entities' disclosures (OGE 278-T) are not available here as dated, machine-readable trade lists, so they aren't ranked; their documented holdings are in the Disclosures tab.

        Methods, terms & sources

        New here? How to read this page in one minute

        1. A "mention" is a public comment by Trump, a family member or an administration official that names a stock, fund, crypto coin or industry, such as praising a company or announcing a government stake. Every one links to where it was said (see the Src column in the All mentions table).
        2. "Beat the index" means the asset did better than the overall market over the same days. The market is the S&P 500 unless you pick another index in the filter bar. A gap of +5% means the asset did 5 percentage points better than the market; −5% means 5 points worse.
        3. Time windows: "Comment day" is the first trading session after the comment could be seen (posts after the 4 PM ET close count from the next day). "+7d", "+30d" and "+90d" are a week, a month and three months later, and "to date" is from the comment until the latest prices.
        4. Who said it is shown the same way everywhere: ● amber circle for Trump, ▲ violet triangle for family, ◆ cyan diamond for administration officials.
        5. Good places to start: the Quick takeaway and Plain-English insights just below, the Newest mentions, the Ticker lookup to look up one company, Congress trades for members' trades near each comment, and the Rankings tab for how members' own disclosed trades did vs the S&P 500. "More than luck?" says whether a pattern is bigger than chance would explain.
        6. What this page is not: it shows timing and price patterns from public records. A pattern, or a trade close to a comment, is not proof that anyone did anything wrong, and nothing here is financial advice.

        What the terms mean

        • First mention only: some assets come up again and again (Bitcoin and Ethereum five times each). Ticking "Count only the first mention of each asset" keeps just the earliest mention of each asset in your filters, so repeat comments, which often come after the price has already moved, don't count several times.
        • Same post: when one post names several assets (for example a single post naming XRP, SOL and ADA), each asset is listed separately. Ticking "Count each post once" averages them into one result so a single post doesn't count three times.
        • Benchmark / index: the "do-nothing" comparison, for example simply owning the whole S&P 500 instead.
        • Total return: price change plus dividends, as if each dividend were reinvested. Index levels like the S&P 500 leave dividends out, so they look slightly worse than actually owning the index. The "total return" benchmarks use index funds (SPY, DIA, QQQ, VTI) with dividends added back.
        • Excess return: how much better (or worse) the mentioned asset did than the benchmark over the same days. +5% means it beat the index by 5 percentage points.
        • Win rate: the share of mentions where the asset's price went up over that period. "Beat the index" is the share that did better than the benchmark.
        • +7d / +30d / +90d / to date: 7, 30 or 90 calendar days after the mention, or from the mention until today.
        • Pre-move (-30d / -7d): how the asset moved in the month or week before the comment, compared with the index.
        • Industry peer ETF: a fund that tracks a narrower slice than a Select Sector SPDR — for example SMH for semiconductors (vs XLK for all technology), URA for uranium, REMX for rare earths/strategic metals, ITA for aerospace & defense. Used as a closer yardstick than the broad sector fund.
        • Basket: for whole-sector comments (e.g. "rare earths"), an equal mix of the related stocks/ETFs listed in that sector's card.
        • 95% range / "luck": how far the average could plausibly swing just from which mentions happened to be in the sample. If the range includes zero, the result could easily be chance.
        • Related committee: in the Congress trades table, a member is marked when they currently sit on a full committee whose work often touches that company's industry (for example, Armed Services for a defense contractor). Seats come from a public dataset of current members and may differ from the trade date. The list of which committee fits which company is a rough guide we wrote from each committee's published jurisdiction. Many members sit on such committees, so this is context, not evidence of anything improper.
        • Adjusting for many checks: if you run lots of luck checks, a few will look "reliable" by accident. The Benjamini-Hochberg correction raises the bar so that, on average, no more than about 5% of the results flagged as reliable are false alarms.
        • Ordinary pre-moves: how often the same asset makes a move as big as the flagged one in the 30 or 7 days before an ordinary day, used to say whether the number of flagged pre-comment moves is more than chance.
        • Random-date (placebo) baseline: the same asset measured on ordinary days with no comment, to see whether a comment-day move is unusual for that asset or just its normal swing.
        • Beta / beta-adjusted: beta is how much an asset tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1% (beta 2 means about 2%). A beta-adjusted (or "abnormal") return subtracts beta × the market's move, so a jumpy stock isn't credited just for riding a rising market.
        • Virtual portfolio: pretend $1,000 went into each mentioned asset at the close on the day it was mentioned.
        • Truth API: a paid data feed Trump Media has sold since Aug 1, 2026 ($100,000 a month) that pushes posts from top Truth Social accounts, including the President's, to subscribers in near real time. Lawsuits allege it gives paying traders a head start; no court has ruled yet.
        • Family-linked listed company: a public stock where Trump, a family member, or a family entity has a documented holding (SEC filing or their own post). Tracked separately from "mentions" so you can see how those companies have moved vs the market around merger filings and other milestones.

        Data & limitations

        Data: Yahoo Finance via yfinance. Asset prices are daily closes, adjusted for splits and dividends. The nine index benchmarks are the index levels themselves (^GSPC, ^DJI, ^IXIC, ^NDX, ^RUT, ^RUI, ^RUA, ^W5000, ^MID), which are price-only, so long-horizon excess is slightly flattered. For a like-for-like comparison, pick a "total return" benchmark (SPY, DIA, QQQ, VTI): fund prices adjusted for dividends, as if dividends were reinvested (minus the funds' small fees). Entry = close on the mention day (or next trading day). Pre windows (-30d, -7d) end at the prior close. "Day 0" = prior close → mention-day close. For posts on Truth Social or X, the exact post time is read from the post's own ID number (both services build the creation time into it) and shown under the date. This works for posts linked directly, and for mentions sourced to news stories or archive pages once the original post has been found on the trumpstruth.org archive (the "post" link in the Src column). Other times come from archives or news reports. When a post or remark comes after the 4 PM ET close (marked "after close" in Flags), the entry moves to the next trading day so Day 0 is the first session that could react; weekend and holiday posts likewise start on the next trading day. Post windows start at the entry close and use calendar days. Sector/theme comments are scored as an equal-weight basket of documented proxies. Disclosure and congressional overlaps are factual timing matches from public filings (amounts are ranges, filings lag trades by up to 30–45+ days). They are not evidence of wrongdoing, and none is alleged. Small, overlapping samples; not investment advice.
