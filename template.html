Plain-English insights (updates with your filters & benchmark)

    What do these terms mean?
    • Same post: when one post names several assets (for example a single post naming XRP, SOL and ADA), each asset is listed separately. Ticking "Count each post once" averages them into one result so a single post doesn't count three times.
    • Benchmark / index: the "do-nothing" comparison, for example simply owning the whole S&P 500 instead.
    • Total return: price change plus dividends, as if each dividend were reinvested. Index levels like the S&P 500 leave dividends out, so they look slightly worse than actually owning the index. The "total return" benchmarks use index funds (SPY, DIA, QQQ, VTI) with dividends added back.
    • Excess return: how much better (or worse) the mentioned asset did than the benchmark over the same days. +5% means it beat the index by 5 percentage points.
    • Win rate: the share of mentions where the asset's price went up over that period. "Beat the index" is the share that did better than the benchmark.
    • +7d / +30d / +90d / to date: 7, 30 or 90 calendar days after the mention, or from the mention until today.
    • Pre-move (-30d / -7d): how the asset moved in the month or week before the comment, compared with the index.
    • Industry peer ETF: a fund that tracks a narrower slice than a Select Sector SPDR — for example SMH for semiconductors (vs XLK for all technology), URA for uranium, REMX for rare earths/strategic metals, ITA for aerospace & defense. Used as a closer yardstick than the broad sector fund.
    • Basket: for whole-sector comments (e.g. "rare earths"), an equal mix of the related stocks/ETFs listed in that sector's card.
    • 95% range / "luck": how far the average could plausibly swing just from which mentions happened to be in the sample. If the range includes zero, the result could easily be chance.
    • Related committee: in the Congress trades table, a member is marked when they currently sit on a full committee whose work often touches that company's industry (for example, Armed Services for a defense contractor). Seats come from a public dataset of current members and may differ from the trade date. The list of which committee fits which company is a rough guide we wrote from each committee's published jurisdiction. Many members sit on such committees, so this is context, not evidence of anything improper.
    • Adjusting for many checks: if you run lots of luck checks, a few will look "reliable" by accident. The Benjamini-Hochberg correction raises the bar so that, on average, no more than about 5% of the results flagged as reliable are false alarms.
    • Ordinary pre-moves: how often the same asset makes a move as big as the flagged one in the 30 or 7 days before an ordinary day, used to say whether the number of flagged pre-comment moves is more than chance.
    • Random-date (placebo) baseline: the same asset measured on ordinary days with no comment, to see whether a comment-day move is unusual for that asset or just its normal swing.
    • Beta / beta-adjusted: beta is how much an asset tends to move when the S&P 500 moves 1% (beta 2 means about 2%). A beta-adjusted (or "abnormal") return subtracts beta × the market's move, so a jumpy stock isn't credited just for riding a rising market.
    • Virtual portfolio: pretend $1,000 went into each mentioned asset at the close on the day it was mentioned.
    • Truth API: a paid data feed Trump Media has sold since Aug 1, 2026 ($100,000 a month) that pushes posts from top Truth Social accounts, including the President's, to subscribers in near real time. Lawsuits allege it gives paying traders a head start; no court has ruled yet.
    • Family-linked listed company: a public stock where Trump, a family member, or a family entity has a documented holding (SEC filing or their own post). Tracked separately from "mentions" so you can see how those companies have moved vs the market around merger filings and other milestones.
    Caveats: past results don't predict future ones. Samples are small and overlap in time. Timing patterns are correlation, not proof of anything. Prices exclude fees and taxes. This is not financial advice.

    Is this more than luck? (average excess vs , with a 95% range)

    For each time window, we resample the mentions 2,000 times (a "bootstrap") to see how much the average could swing by chance. If the 95% range crosses zero, the result can't be told apart from luck. The coin-flip check asks whether the share of mentions beating the index is unusually far from 50%. Small, overlapping samples make these ranges wide.

    Did the stock beat its own industry? (vs sector fund, and vs a tighter industry peer ETF)

    A stock can beat the S&P 500 just because its whole sector was rising. First we compare each mentioned stock with its Select Sector SPDR fund (for example chip stocks vs the tech fund XLK). Then, where a tighter industry ETF exists, we also compare it with that peer (for example chips vs the semiconductor fund SMH, uranium vs URA, rare earths vs REMX). The peer is a closer yardstick for "did this company stand out from its own niche?"

    Adjusting for how jumpy each asset is (beta-adjusted returns vs the S&P 500)

    Some stocks swing much more than the market (a "high-beta" stock might move 2% when the S&P 500 moves 1%). Simply subtracting the index can make such a stock look good in a rising market or bad in a falling one. Here each asset's typical sensitivity to the S&P 500 (its beta) is measured from about a year of daily prices ending roughly six weeks before the comment, and the expected market-driven part (beta × the S&P 500's move) is subtracted. What's left is the move the market alone doesn't explain. Always measured against the S&P 500, whichever benchmark is selected above.

    Compared with ordinary days (random-date baseline for the same assets, vs the S&P 500)

    A fair question about any jump is: "does this asset do that all the time?" Here each mentioned asset is compared with itself on ordinary days. For every asset we take its trading days from two years before the comment up to today, leave out the month either side of any tracked comment on it, and measure how it did against the S&P 500 on those days. Then we pretend to "mention" each asset on a random ordinary day, 2,000 times over, to see what a normal average looks like. If the real comment-day average sits outside that normal range, chance and everyday volatility are unlikely to explain it. Each mention counts separately here (the "count each post once" box doesn't apply), and it is always measured against the S&P 500.

    Family-linked listed companies (context: share price vs indexes + SEC milestones)

    These are companies where Trump family members or family-linked entities have a documented public equity stake (SEC 13D/13G, company filings, or their own posts). This is market context, not a trading tip and not an allegation. A holding or a price move does not mean anyone broke a law.

    Paid early access to Trump's posts ("Truth API") (context: what it is, and how mentions since its launch compare; vs )

    Since August 1, 2026, Trump Media (the company behind Truth Social, whose largest shareholder is a trust of President Trump's) has sold a data feed called the Truth API. It costs $100,000 a month (or $60,000 a month on a three-year commitment) and pushes posts from top accounts, including President Trump's, the White House's, Dan Scavino's, RFK Jr.'s, Sean Duffy's and Kash Patel's, to subscribers in near real time. Several lawsuits challenge it: two nonprofits sued in federal court in Washington, D.C. on Sept 17, San Francisco sued under California's unfair-competition law on Sept 23, and a New York federal judge heard a request to pause it on Oct 7 and has not yet ruled. The lawsuits allege subscribers get government news before everyone else; at the hearing the judge asked whether the feed is only about 50 milliseconds faster, and the defence argued a story arriving a bit later is not real harm. None of this has been decided by a court.
